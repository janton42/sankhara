    Concepts¶

    The design of Sankhara. How each part is built is in Architecture.

    Premise¶

    A simulation of one people over many generations, played one life at a time. It is a nation builder in the spirit of Civilization's development (ideas that take years, plans that run until something interrupts them) and of Europa Universalis' leaders (people who are born, rule and die), but war and armies are not the point. Play is about food and stores, custom and government, and the people's ideas and culture.

    Buddhist ideas (saṅkhāra, karma, rebirth) shape the mechanics but are never named or explained in the game, the README, the About text or any other description. Nothing says that there is no winning: the game simply runs until the player stops.

    The start¶

    The people begin as one small band, the first of their kind, a few hundred people at most (never more than about 1,500) in a few camps. They have fire, stone tools, names, a language, old songs and some belief in the unseen. There is no farming, trade, money, goods, cities or armies, and no settled way of choosing leaders.

    Development¶

    Everything beyond that must be discovered, and nothing is prescribed to turn out as it did in the real world. Ideas form a web: each needs earlier ideas and the right circumstances (water nearby, grassland, game, having met another people, enough people). Insight comes from ordinary life, from someone working at an idea, from chance and from other peoples. A full web should take a few hundred years.

    Many ideas are customs that settle into one of several forms with different effects: how giving works, what exchange uses (favours owed, wares, tokens of shell or stone, or none of these), how planting is done, how leaders are chosen, how wrongs are judged. Whoever worked an idea out, or the leader, chooses its form; when a custom is being settled anyone may speak for a form, and the respected are heard more. Otherwise the people's own inclinations decide.

    Roles appear with the ideas that make them: leader, keeper of the songs, hunter, gatherer and maker at first; fisher, planter, herder, go-between and others later, while old ways grow rarer.

    A year¶

    One turn is one year. The years run on a clock the player can pause or step through one at a time; each year lasts long enough to react. News (something expected of you, a work finished or stalled, a discovery, strangers, a death) is shown and lingers but does not stop the years. Only a pop-up asking the player to decide holds them.

    The player acts as their current character, not as an abstract manager. They have:

    • Three slots of attention, all optional. Nothing is chosen for the player, and a whole life can pass with nothing taken up.
    • Their role's means: a coarse budget of a few kinds (food, hands, regard, and later whatever form of exchange the people settle on). The leader also draws on what is held in common; others on their family's.

    Attention¶

    What a slot can hold comes in three groups, so there are only a dozen or so things to weigh in a year rather than a long menu:

    • Matters at hand: situations drawn from the people's real state and the year's events: a hungry camp, quarrels between named families, low stores or plenty, a stranger at the fire, a dying elder, a raid, talk that you should lead. Each role meets some of their own too (a hunter's share of a great kill, a keeper asked for the oldest songs, a maker asked for a blade). A matter offers two or three ways to meet it, which depend on the role: the leader can open the common stores to the hungry, a hunter can bring them meat, anyone can share their family's food or keep it close. Some matters are expected of the player; ignoring those weighs on the life, and any answer meets them. An answer is kept up for as long as the matter lasts.
    • The people's works: begin, help with, take up or call others to them (see below). A work holds its slot until it is finished, set aside or stalls, and then the slot is left free.
    • Your own: personal undertakings (raising a child, an apprentice, a following, a shelter) and lasting cares (your kin, your craft, the songs, the unseen), kept up until changed.

    No choice is labelled good or bad. Taking more than your share, shaming someone, hoarding knowledge or driving a hard bargain never appear as items on a menu; they come up only as one way of meeting a real situation, next to the others. Regard is earned by serving the people: tending them, keeping their songs, and above all leading or helping a work of theirs to the end.

    Leading from anywhere¶

    Most projects are the people's works: shared undertakings that outlive whoever began them. Anyone can begin one, help with one, take up one left unfinished, or call others to it; when the one leading it dies it passes on rather than ending. Together with speaking when customs are settled, this gives every role a clear, connected influence over the direction of the people, even though most lives will not be the leader's.

    The thread between lives runs through these works: a new life opens with what was finished while the character grew up, and what is still unfinished and who carries it.

    Leadership¶

    In a society this young leadership is earned, not inherited. The people look to whoever is most respected; a leader may give way when old or frail, or be passed over for someone who far outshines them, and anyone respected enough can ask the people to follow them (and may fail). The first character leads for a random reason of their own (bringing the hunters home through the snows, settling a quarrel...), never because a parent died. Only if the people adopt a custom of lineage does leading pass through a family; a council of elders or the strongest are other forms.

    Events¶

    Each year brings events, some random and some from the people's state. They are not fair: outcomes can be unjust or pointless. Events are the main source of obligations, which ask something of the player and may have harsher alternative answers.

    Lives and rebirth¶

    A life runs about 20 to 50 years from adulthood, with a hidden, random death age (health can end it sooner), so final decisions cannot be timed. A game is several hundred years and many lives.

    On death the player is reborn among the same people, into a role drawn from those that exist. Rank is not a reward: every role has the same three slots, with different kinds and scope of influence (a leader's broad and slow, a fisher's local and immediate). Status says nothing about worth. The people the player shaped remain, and they may be born into the consequences.

    Karma¶

    A hidden score in [-1, 1], never shown as a number or a word; only its effects are felt. It attaches to intention and choice, never to outcomes or to the budget: which answers, cares and works are taken up, which expected matters are ignored, which forms are chosen. Stepping through a year attentively weighs slightly well; letting a year slip by with nothing taken up weighs slightly ill.

    At rebirth karma shapes the conditions within the new role, not the role itself: mostly the inclinations carried over, and to a lesser degree health, fortune, family stability and the warmth of parents.

    Tendencies (saṅkhāra)¶

    A life's habitual choices become the next life's default impulses. They are never shown as labels or a character sheet, only felt: some choices cost less, come first or are tinted as tempting; others carry friction. The nudge is mild to moderate, always overridable at a small cost, and consistent enough to be learned. Repetition strengthens a tendency, acting against it weakens it, and the pattern at death carries over.

    Other people¶

    NPCs follow the same model, simplified. The leader, elders and people near the player or in power are tracked in detail; the rest are handled statistically.

    Other peoples share the land. They grow, learn, meet the player's people, share ideas, intermarry, and drift toward or away from them.

    Violence¶

    Violence is real and should be present, but never the focus. For now it comes as raids from other peoples, answered by seeking redress or, more harshly, by revenge. There are no armies; any form of organised violence must be discovered like everything else.
